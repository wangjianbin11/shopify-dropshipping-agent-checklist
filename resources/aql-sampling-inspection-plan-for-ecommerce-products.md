# AQL Sampling Inspection Plan for Ecommerce Products

AQL sampling gives ecommerce operators a structured way to assess a production lot. It is not 100 percent inspection, and it cannot guarantee that every defect will be found.

Use this plan before production or pre-shipment inspection so the supplier, inspector, fulfillment team, and buyer are working from the same written rules.

## 1. Define the Product and Lot

Record the exact scope before choosing a sample.

- Product name and SKU
- Purchase order or batch reference
- Total lot quantity
- Color, size, material, model, and variant mix
- Approved sample, specification sheet, or artwork revision
- Packaging and labeling requirements
- Target market and any market-specific requirements

Do not combine different products or materially different variants into one inspection lot unless the sampling plan explicitly allows it.

## 2. Define Defect Classes

Write product-specific examples for each class. The standard does not decide these examples for you.

### Critical

A condition that may create an unreasonable safety, legal, or regulatory risk, or make the product unsafe to use.

Examples must be defined for the actual product. A cosmetic issue should not be labeled critical simply because it is visible.

### Major

A defect likely to cause a return, complaint, functional failure, or material departure from the approved specification.

### Minor

A smaller departure that does not materially affect intended use but still falls outside the agreed appearance or workmanship standard.

## 3. Choose the Sampling Framework

Document the framework and revision used. ISO 2859-1 and ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 are common acceptance-sampling frameworks for inspection by attributes.

Record:

- Inspection type: normal, tightened, or reduced
- Inspection level
- Lot-size range
- Sample-size code letter
- Sample size
- Acceptance and rejection numbers for each defect class

General Inspection Level II is commonly used for routine consumer-goods inspection, but it is not automatically correct for every product. Product risk, supplier history, process stability, customer requirements, and inspection cost can justify a different plan.

## 5. Define the Inspection Checks

Build the checklist from the actual product specification.

- Quantity and variant mix
- Product identity and dimensions
- Materials, color, finish, and workmanship
- Function and basic performance
- Assembly and included components
- Barcode, label, insert, and language requirements
- Packaging condition and pack-out method
- Carton quantity, markings, and shipping labels
- Photos or video required as evidence

Each check should have an expected result, test method, sample quantity, and evidence requirement.

## 6. Record Evidence Consistently

The inspection record should be auditable after the goods leave the factory.

Capture:

- Inspection date and location
- Inspector or responsible party
- Lot quantity and sampled quantity
- Defects by class with photos
- Test results and measurements
- Packaging and label evidence
- Acceptance or rejection decision
- Any deviation approved by the buyer
- Corrective action, owner, and deadline

Avoid a report that only says "passed." The record should show what was checked and what evidence supports the decision.

## 7. Decide the Lot Disposition

Define the possible outcomes before inspection:

- Accept the lot
- Hold for buyer review
- Rework affected units
- Replace defective units
- Perform 100 percent sorting
- Reinspect after corrective action
- Reject the lot

A sampling result is a lot-acceptance decision under the selected plan. It is not a promise that every unit is defect-free.

## 8. Know When Sampling Is Not Enough

Sampling may be inappropriate or insufficient when:

- A defect could create a serious safety or compliance risk
- The buyer or regulator requires every unit to be checked
- The process is unstable or the supplier has repeated failures
- The product requires laboratory, performance, or certification testing
- Mixed or untraceable lots prevent a valid sample
- A previous rejection requires containment or 100 percent sorting

Escalate these cases to the appropriate quality, compliance, or testing specialist.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Treating AQL sampling as full inspection
- Selecting AQL values without product-specific risk review
- Leaving critical, major, and minor defects undefined
- Inspecting against a product link instead of an approved specification
- Mixing different variants or batches without a documented rule
- Accepting a pass/fail statement with no defect and evidence record
- Shipping a rejected or held lot before corrective action is approved
